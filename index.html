I teach people how to work with data for a living — and I analyze data the same way I teach it: start with the business question, not the dataset.

As a Data Analytics Instructor, I spend every day doing the thing most analysts skip: making SQL and data concepts click for people who've never queried a database before. That habit shapes how I work — I clean data carefully, document every step, and build dashboards a non-technical manager can read in ten seconds.

Before teaching, I worked in customer support, which is where I actually learned to read data — every ticket, complaint, and repeat question was a signal about what the business needed to fix. That experience is why I don't just build charts; I look for the decision a chart should drive.

I'm continuing to build on SQL, Python, Excel, and Power BI, and I document every project the way I'd want a student to see it: the problem, the process, and the result — nothing hidden, nothing assumed.

Every project answers the same five questions: what was broken, what I did, what I found, what decision it enables, and why that matters to the business. The three below are formatted examples — swap in your own dataset, numbers, and dashboard once built.

Customer Churn Dashboard
Sample — replace with real data
01
Business Problem
Subscription business couldn't tell which customers were about to cancel, so retention offers went out too late — or to the wrong people.
02
Dataset
12 months of subscriber records: plan type, usage frequency, support tickets, billing history.
03
Analysis
Cleaned and joined billing + usage tables in SQL; built churn-risk segments in Python (pandas) based on usage drop-off and ticket volume.
04
Dashboard
Power BI dashboard: churn rate by segment, at-risk customer list, retention-offer performance.
05
Insight
Customers who filed 2+ support tickets in a month churned at a far higher rate than any other segment — a clearer warning sign than usage decline alone.
Business impact: Support-ticket volume becomes a leading churn indicator, so retention outreach can trigger weeks earlier — before the customer decides to leave.

Online Retail Sales Analysis
View on GitHub ↗
01
Business Problem
A UK-based online giftware retailer had 536,500 raw transaction records but no way to say which customers or products actually drove revenue, or whether the business was seasonal, growing, or data-quality-compromised.
02
Dataset
13 months of transaction line items (Dec 2010 – Dec 2011): invoice, product, quantity, price, customer ID, country — 4,372 customers, 3,940 products, 38 countries (UCI Online Retail dataset).
03
Analysis
Cleaned and documented every transformation in Python/pandas (flag-rather-than-delete approach), then ran EDA on revenue trends, seasonality, customer concentration, and returns.
04
Deliverables
Cleaning log, 7 EDA charts, 9 quantified insights, 6 business recommendations, and an 11-slide stakeholder presentation with full speaker notes.
05
Insight
The top 20% of customers generated ~74.6% of revenue and November alone drove ~101% more revenue than the average month — while 12.6% of revenue couldn't be tied to any customer due to missing IDs.
Business impact: Quantified exactly where Q4 staffing, a loyalty program for top-tier customers, and checkout data-capture fixes would move revenue — full detail in the recommendations report.

Customer Support Analytics
Sample — replace with real data
01
Business Problem
Support team knew ticket volume was rising but couldn't say why, or which issue types were driving repeat contacts.
02
Dataset
Support ticket logs: category, resolution time, agent, channel, repeat-contact flag.
03
Analysis
Cleaned free-text category tags into a consistent taxonomy, then measured resolution time and repeat-contact rate per category in Python.
04
Dashboard
Power BI dashboard: ticket volume by category and channel, average resolution time, repeat-contact rate.
05
Insight
One issue category had an average resolution time twice the team benchmark, and it accounted for a disproportionate share of repeat contacts.
Business impact: Support leadership can route training and staffing to the one category creating the most repeat work, instead of spreading fixes evenly across all of them.
